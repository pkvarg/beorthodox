The charge is familiar. Take away the pope and the councils, and every believer becomes his own pope. Each one reads the Bible alone, decides alone what it means, and the result is <span class="article-body_accent">thousands of denominations.</span>

A verse is usually quoted with it: “no prophecy of the scripture is of any private interpretation” (2 Pet 1:20).

## What the verse says

Read the next verse. “For the prophecy came not in old time by the will of man: but holy men of God spake as they were moved by the Holy Ghost” (2 Pet 1:21). Peter is speaking about where prophecy came from, not about who is allowed to read it. The prophets did not make it up.

So the verse does not settle the question. The charge still has to be answered.

## What is true in the charge

A good deal of it is true, and it should be admitted first.

Many believers do live by “me and my Bible”. Many congregations are built around one gifted man whose word is final, a pope in all but name. Groups divide over baptism, over the end times, over music. And many who say “the Bible only” quietly borrow the old creeds without knowing where they came from.

<p class="article-body_highlight">A man alone with his Bible, answerable to no one, really is his own pope.</p>

But that was never the teaching that Scripture is the highest authority. It is a caricature of it. Scripture alone as the final judge is one thing. A believer alone as the final judge is another. The Reformers quoted the early teachers of the church constantly.

<p class="article-body_highlight">What they rejected was not history, but the claim that any tradition cannot err.</p>

## Scripture sets its own priorities

Who decides what is essential? The usual answer is the saying “in essentials unity, in non-essentials liberty”, often credited to Augustine, though it comes from the seventeenth century. But we do not have to decide. Scripture tells us what it holds first.

- <strong class="article-body_accent">Of first importance.</strong> Christ died for our sins, was buried and rose again the third day (1 Cor 15:3–4).
- <strong class="article-body_accent">The person of Christ.</strong> Jesus Christ is come in the flesh (1 John 4:2–3; 2 John 7–10).
- <strong class="article-body_accent">The gospel of grace.</strong> Any other gospel is to be refused (Gal 1:8–9).
- <strong class="article-body_accent">A holy life.</strong> Open sin is a reason to refuse fellowship (1 Cor 5:11).
- <strong class="article-body_accent">Disputable matters.</strong> Food and days are left to each one’s conscience (Rom 14:1–6).

## Reading with all the saints

The New Testament never pictures a believer understanding alone. We comprehend <span class="article-body_accent">“with all saints”</span> (Eph 3:18). Every believer has the anointing that teaches (1 John 2:20, 27), and when one speaks, the others weigh what is said (1 Cor 14:29).

Acts 15 shows it at work. A real dispute arose. The apostles and elders came together, heard the testimony, and James settled it by quoting the prophets (Acts 15:15–17).

<p class="article-body_highlight">The church decided by reasoning from Scripture, not by pointing to an office.</p>

## The charge turns back

Someone who leaves for Rome or Constantinople has also made a choice. He read, compared, weighed the arguments and decided which church to trust. That too is private judgment, and no one can avoid it.

Nor does it end there. The decrees of councils and popes are also texts, and they also need interpreting. And the ancient churches do not agree among themselves: not about the pope, not about the creed, not even about the calendar.

<p class="article-body_highlight">The cure for every man being his own pope is not one pope for all. It is the Body under its Head.</p>

Christ is the Head, the Scriptures are his word, and we learn to hear them together.
