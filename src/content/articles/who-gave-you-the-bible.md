Almost every story of someone leaving for Rome or Constantinople reaches the same moment. Somebody asks, “Who gave you the Bible?” and answers for you: “The Church did.”

The argument runs like this. The Church decided which books belong in the Bible. So the Church stands above the Bible, or at least beside it. So you must accept what the Church teaches, including what it taught later.

It is a serious argument and it deserves a serious answer. The real aim is the last step: accept our authority, and with it our later teachings.

## God gave the Bible

<p class="article-body_highlight">God gave the Scriptures. The church received them.</p>

All Scripture is God-breathed (2 Tim 3:16). No prophecy came by the will of man; men spoke from God as they were carried along by the Holy Spirit (2 Pet 1:20–21). The household of God is built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets (Eph 2:20), <span class="article-body_accent">not on those who came after them.</span>

The books were also recognized long before any council met. Peter counts Paul’s letters with “the other Scriptures” (2 Pet 3:15–16). Paul quotes a saying found in Luke’s Gospel as Scripture (1 Tim 5:18; Luke 10:7). This happened while the apostles were still alive.

## Written to the churches, not by them

The letters of the New Testament are addressed to churches: to the church of God in Corinth (1 Cor 1:2), to the churches of Galatia (Gal 1:2), to the seven churches in Asia (Rev 1:4, 11). They were read aloud and passed on from one church to the next (Col 4:16; 1 Thess 5:27). “He that hath an ear, let him hear what the Spirit saith unto the churches” (Rev 2:7).

The one who receives a letter is not its author. A letter carrier gains no authority over the letter he delivers.

Israel is the clearest example. The Jews were entrusted with the oracles of God (Rom 3:2). Yet the Lord Jesus judged their leaders by those same Scriptures (Mark 7:8–13; Matt 22:29).

<p class="article-body_highlight">To keep the Scriptures is not to be above them.</p>

## Recognized, not created

<p class="article-body_highlight">The church recognized the canon. It did not create it.</p>

“My sheep hear my voice” (John 10:27). A child recognizes its mother, and that does not make the child the mother.

History says the same. The first list that matches our twenty-seven New Testament books is in a letter Athanasius wrote in 367. The synods of Hippo (393) and Carthage (397) were regional gatherings. For Roman Catholics the list was defined as dogma only at the Council of Trent in 1546. The Orthodox churches have never issued one binding list, and their Bibles do not all contain the same books.

So which Church gave us which Bible? The books were already being read, copied and obeyed across the churches for centuries <span class="article-body_accent">before anyone ruled on them.</span>

## Scripture judges every teacher

If the Scriptures come from God, then no institution may teach against them. Paul says that even an apostle or an angel who brings another gospel is to be rejected (Gal 1:8–9). The Bereans were praised for testing Paul’s own preaching by the Scriptures (Acts 17:11). “Prove all things; hold fast that which is good” (1 Thess 5:21).

This test cuts in every direction.

- <strong class="article-body_accent">Titles.</strong> The Lord told his disciples to call no man on earth father or teacher in this way, “all ye are brethren” (Matt 23:8–10). That speaks to “Father”, and just as much to “Reverend” and “Pastor”.
- <strong class="article-body_accent">Priests.</strong> The New Testament never calls elders priests. It calls all believers a royal priesthood (1 Pet 2:9).
- <strong class="article-body_accent">Mediators.</strong> There is one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus (1 Tim 2:5).

We owe a great deal to the believers who copied, guarded and handed on these books, often at the cost of their lives. We honour them best by doing what they did: hearing the Scriptures as the voice of God, and letting them judge every one of us.
