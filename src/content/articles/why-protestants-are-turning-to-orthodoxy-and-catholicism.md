On Palm Sunday, 9 April 2017, Hank Hanegraaff was received into the Greek Orthodox Church at St. Nektarios in Charlotte, North Carolina, together with his wife and two of their children. For decades he had been known on American radio as “the Bible Answer Man”. Within days a radio network dropped his programme.

Why would a man whose whole ministry was answering from the Bible, and who knew what it would cost him, look for the answer in an ancient church?

Perhaps you know someone who left your fellowship for Rome or for Constantinople. Perhaps you have felt the pull yourself.

## What is happening

He was not the first. John Henry Newman left the Church of England for Rome in 1845. Thomas Howard, an English professor at an evangelical college, became a Catholic in 1985. Scott Hahn, a former Presbyterian minister, followed in 1986. In 1987 Peter Gillquist led a whole group of evangelical congregations into the Orthodox Church. Francis Beckwith was president of the Evangelical Theological Society when he returned to the Catholic Church in 2007. In 2014 Ulf Ekman, founder of the charismatic Word of Life church in Uppsala, Sweden, became a Catholic with his wife.

Today the movement is louder than ever. It lives in podcasts, in online debates and in conversion stories on video, and it speaks especially to young men.

It is also smaller than the headlines suggest. Pew Research Center found that converts to Catholicism are about 1.5% of adults in the United States, and that for every person who joins, more than eight leave. For Orthodoxy there are no reliable figures at all. The trend is real, but it is not a flood.

## Five reasons, heard fairly

People who make this move give reasons, and there is something true in each of them.

- <strong class="article-body_accent">History.</strong> Newman wrote, “To be deep in history is to cease to be a Protestant.” It is true that many Protestants know almost nothing between the book of Acts and Luther.
- <strong class="article-body_accent">Authority.</strong> They see thousands of denominations, each with its own interpretation, and want a church that can say what is true. The division and the individualism are real.
- <strong class="article-body_accent">Worship.</strong> They want reverence and beauty instead of entertainment and a stage. Much Protestant worship is shallow and made for consumers.
- <strong class="article-body_accent">The Lord’s Table.</strong> They want a real meeting with Christ, not a symbol taken a few times a year. In many places the Table has been pushed to the margins.
- <strong class="article-body_accent">Depth.</strong> They want a faith that transforms a person, not only a ticket to heaven. Protestant teaching often stops at being forgiven.

## What they have in common

Every one of these reasons is about the church. These people are not leaving Christ. They are looking for the church they never found.

That hunger is right. Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her (Eph 5:25–27), and God makes his wisdom known through the church (Eph 3:10).

<p class="article-body_highlight">The Reformation recovered how a person is saved, but it left the question of what the church is largely untouched. Those who leave have noticed the gap.</p>

## The question

So the question is this: is the answer to that hunger

<p class="article-body_highlight">the oldest institution, or the original pattern given in Scripture?</p>

The faith was delivered once for all to the saints (Jude 3). Scripture is enough to make the man of God complete (2 Tim 3:16–17). The first believers continued in the apostles’ teaching and fellowship, in the breaking of bread and in prayers (Acts 2:42). That is the measuring line.

We are not attacking anyone who has made this move, and we do not doubt their sincerity. Many of them are reacting to real failures, and that has to be heard first. We want to speak the truth in love (Eph 4:15), with gentleness and respect (1 Pet 3:15).

If you have felt the pull toward an ancient church, what exactly were you looking for?
